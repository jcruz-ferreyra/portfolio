Environmental Sensor Network Pipeline

  • Live Site

A cloud data pipeline supporting a community-led environmental justice initiative in Roxbury and Dorchester, processing real-time heat, and noise readings from 50+ distributed sensors across the Blue Hill Avenue corridor.

Context
Common SENSES is a community-led action research project supporting environmental justice along Blue Hill Avenue, from Dudley Town Common to Franklin Park. Led by the Dudley Street Neighborhood Initiative, Project RIGHT, BARI, and the City of Boston's Office of Emerging Technology, the initiative deploys environmental sensors as community infrastructure across the Roxbury and Dorchester neighborhoods, making data openly available for advocacy, policy conversations, and co-designed solutions. Figure 1 shows the geographic distribution of sensors along the corridor.

Data
The pipeline ingests real-time heat and noise readings from 50+ distributed sensors through a webhook-based architecture. Raw data is processed and staged in Azure Blob Storage before being loaded into a normalized relational database, ensuring consistent structure across sensor types and deployment locations. Figure 3 illustrates the overall pipeline architecture from ingestion to storage.

Findings
The deployed system processes sensor readings continuously and makes them accessible through two distinct channels: researchers access the database directly through read permissions, while community members can explore live and historical readings through an interactive dashboard embedded in an ArcGIS map. Figure 2 shows the dashboard interface, which allows anyone to track conditions at any sensor location across the corridor without technical expertise.

Implications
By translating raw environmental data into an accessible public resource, the system enables residents to document conditions in their own neighborhood and engage in evidence-based advocacy around heat and noise. The infrastructure also provides a scalable template for extending community-facing environmental monitoring to other corridors across Boston.

Bluebikes Network Expansion: A GIS Siting Analysis

  • Report

A GIS analysis identifying underserved areas in Boston's Bluebikes network and recommending three new station locations based on population density, bike infrastructure proximity, and existing ridership demand.

Context
Boston has invested meaningfully in cycling infrastructure in recent years, yet gaps remain in the Bluebikes bike-share network. Well-placed stations improve accessibility, close service gaps in underserved neighborhoods, and support broader sustainability goals by encouraging active transportation. This project uses open geospatial data to identify where new stations could most effectively expand access across the city. Figure 1 provides an overview of the current network coverage.

Data
All data comes from publicly available sources: trip records and station coordinates from the Bluebikes website, 2020 U.S. Census population counts at the census block level and bike lane shapefiles from Analyze Boston, street network data from TIGER/Line shapefiles, and Boston geography files from the BARI Harvard Dataverse. The analysis was conducted entirely with open data, underscoring the value of civic data infrastructure for urban research.

Findings
A network-based walkability assessment maps coverage at 200, 500, and 1,000 meter walking distances using actual street geometry rather than straight-line buffers. Ten preliminary candidate sites are identified through visual analysis of coverage gaps overlaid with population data (Figures 2 and 3). Each site is then evaluated against three measurable criteria: 500 or more residents within a 200 meter buffer (Figure 4), proximity to a bike lane within 500 meters (Figure 5), and a nearest station with at least 1,000 trips in 2024 (Figure 6). Three sites in Brighton, East Boston, and Roxbury met all criteria (Figure 7).

Implications
The selected sites represent geographically diverse opportunities to extend the network in areas with demonstrated demand and good infrastructure connectivity. The framework developed here offers a replicable, data-driven approach to bike-share planning that could be strengthened further with demographic data, station rebalancing records, and community input.

Developed as part of the GIS for Urban and Regional Planning course at Northeastern University.

Neighborhood Resilience in Boston's Restaurant Sector

  • Report

An analysis of Boston's food establishment inspection records as a proxy for neighborhood restaurant activity over time. Using spatial clustering and an adjusted growth rate metric, the project traces pandemic-era decline and uneven recovery across Boston's communities.

Context
Restaurants are more than places to eat — they reflect the economic health, cultural vitality, and demographic dynamics of the neighborhoods they serve. Boston's food establishment inspection dataset, while designed for regulatory purposes, offers a window into these dynamics when analyzed over time. This project repurposes over 800,000 inspection records to construct a measure of restaurant activity across space and time, with particular attention to the Common SENSES study area along Blue Hill Avenue in Roxbury and Dorchester.

Data
The dataset covers inspections conducted in Boston since 2006 and includes spatial coordinates for each establishment. Rather than relying on administrative boundaries, restaurants are grouped into spatial clusters using the DBSCAN algorithm, which identifies natural concentrations of activity within 250 meters. This approach avoids splitting clusters that straddle neighborhood boundaries and produces more meaningful units for comparison (Figures 1 and 5). An adjusted log growth rate comparing each year's count to a five-year rolling baseline serves as the primary metric, smoothing out short-term anomalies and capturing longer-term trends (Figure 2).

Findings
The 2020 pandemic produced a sharp city-wide decline, with an average growth rate of -0.218 across Boston neighborhoods. Central areas like the West End, Downtown, and Fenway were hit hardest, while peripheral residential neighborhoods proved more resilient. A statistically significant correlation of 0.315 between distance to downtown and 2020 growth rate supports the interpretation that neighborhoods relying on local rather than transient clientele fared better (Figures 3 and 4). At the hyperlocal scale, the Common SENSES area showed notable resilience, reaching pre-pandemic levels by 2021 and recording its highest restaurant count in a decade by 2023, alongside an emerging concentration of shared kitchen facilities serving mobile food vendors (Figures 6, 7, and 8).

Implications
The growth rate metric proved robust across spatial scales, from city-wide neighborhoods down to individual clusters. Rather than offering definitive conclusions, it functions as a complementary lens for understanding urban dynamics, one that becomes more powerful when combined with demographic, economic, or infrastructure data. Future work could integrate variables such as 311 service requests, building permits, or population composition to deepen the narrative of neighborhood change.

Developed as part of the Big Data for Cities course at Northeastern University.
