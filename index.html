Environmental Sensor Network Pipeline

  • Live Site

A cloud data pipeline supporting a community-led environmental justice initiative in Roxbury and Dorchester, processing real-time heat, and noise readings from 50+ distributed sensors across the Blue Hill Avenue corridor.

Common SENSES is a community-led action research project supporting environmental justice along Blue Hill Avenue, from Dudley Town Common to Franklin Park. Led by the Dudley Street Neighborhood Initiative, Project RIGHT, BARI, and the City of Boston's Office of Emerging Technology, the initiative deploys environmental sensors as community infrastructure, making data openly available for neighborhood advocacy, policy conversations, and co-designed solutions.

As a Research Assistant at BARI, I designed and deployed the end-to-end data pipeline supporting this network. The system ingests real-time readings from 50+ sensors through a webhook-based architecture, processes and stores data in Azure Blob Storage, and loads it into a normalized relational database. Researchers access the data directly through the database, while community members can explore live and historical readings through an interactive dashboard embedded in an ArcGIS map showing each sensor's location across the corridor.

The result is a system that translates raw environmental data into an accessible public resource, enabling residents to track conditions in their own neighborhood and engage in evidence-based advocacy around heat, and noise.

Bluebikes Network Expansion: A GIS Siting Analysis

  • Report

A GIS analysis identifying underserved areas in Boston's Bluebikes network and recommending three new station locations based on population density, bike infrastructure proximity, and existing ridership demand.

Boston has made meaningful investments in cycling infrastructure in recent years, but gaps remain in the Bluebikes bike-share network. This project uses open geospatial data to identify where new stations could most effectively expand access, focusing on areas where service gaps overlap with residential density and existing infrastructure.

The analysis begins with a network-based walkability assessment, mapping coverage at 200, 500, and 1,000 meter walking distances from each station using actual street geometry rather than straight-line buffers. Areas beyond 500 meters from any station are treated as underserved. Ten preliminary candidate sites are identified through visual analysis of these coverage gaps overlaid with census block population data.

Each candidate site is then evaluated against three measurable criteria: a minimum of 500 residents within a 200 meter buffer, proximity to an existing bike lane within 500 meters, and a nearest station with at least 1,000 trips recorded in 2024. Sites are further refined through a qualitative review combining GIS data with satellite imagery to assess terrain, street conditions, and local feasibility.

Three final sites in Brighton, East Boston, and Roxbury met all criteria, representing geographically diverse opportunities to extend the network in areas with demonstrated demand and good infrastructure connectivity. All analysis was conducted using publicly available data from Bluebikes, the City of Boston, the U.S. Census, and BARI.

Developed as part of the GIS for Urban and Regional Planning course at Northeastern University.

Neighborhood Resilience in Boston's Restaurant Sector

  • Report

An analysis of Boston's food establishment inspection records as a proxy for neighborhood restaurant activity over time. Using spatial clustering and an adjusted growth rate metric, the project traces pandemic-era decline and uneven recovery across Boston's communities.

Boston's food establishment inspection dataset, while designed for regulatory purposes, holds a richer story about neighborhood vitality. This project transforms over 800,000 inspection records into a measure of restaurant activity across space and time, using DBSCAN clustering to define natural spatial units and an adjusted log growth rate to track changes relative to a rolling baseline.

The analysis surfaces clear pandemic-era patterns: central neighborhoods like the West End, Downtown, and Fenway experienced the steepest declines in 2020, while peripheral, residential neighborhoods proved more resilient, a difference explained in part by their reliance on local rather than transient clientele. A statistically significant correlation between distance to downtown and 2020 growth rate supports this interpretation.

At the hyperlocal scale, the analysis zooms into the Common SENSES study area, a collaborative research initiative focused on Environmental Justice communities along Blue Hill Avenue in Roxbury and Dorchester, revealing thirteen restaurant clusters, notable resilience during the pandemic, and an emerging concentration of shared kitchen facilities serving mobile food vendors.

Developed as part of the Big Data for Cities course at Northeastern University.
